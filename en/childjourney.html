Childjourney

A serious game for children with juvenile dermatomyositis, built so they can do their exercises at home instead of at the hospital.

Role
Game developer, designer & SVG animator
Period
March – July 2025
Stack
React, TypeScript, SVG
Context
Data Intelligence research group & Limburg Meet Zorg (LIME)

The problem

Juvenile dermatomyositis is a rare autoimmune disease that affects the muscles and skin. In the Netherlands, it's actively treated by a single hospital. That means families sometimes travel from the other side of the country for exercises that, technically, could also be done at home.

Except doing exercises at home is hard. They're boring, sometimes painful, and there's no physiotherapist standing next to you cheering you on. Exactly the kind of behaviour that falls apart after two weeks — and with this condition, sticking with it is exactly what matters.

The design question: how do you turn an exercise schedule into something a child goes to on their own, without the game making the exercise feel unimportant?

Why a web game

The first real choice in this project wasn't about gameplay, but about technology. An app in a store means installing, updating, and a device that can handle it. A console means the family needs to own one. Anything standing between the child and the exercise is a reason not to do it.

That's why Childjourney is built as a web game in React and TypeScript: open a link and play. No installation, no extra hardware, works on the laptop or tablet that's already there. For the animation I chose SVG — sharp on any screen, light enough for older devices, and directly controllable from code.

What I built

I worked in a small team and was responsible for the game concept, the design, the implementation and the animation. In short:

  • The game concept: translating an exercise schedule into something playable.
  • The front-end in React and TypeScript, structured so exercises stay decoupled from the game logic.
  • The SVG animations, from character to feedback on a completed exercise.
  • The visual design, tuned to the age group and to what's appropriate in a healthcare context.

What I learned

Childjourney taught me that designing a game for children in a healthcare context is about far more than just making something fun. It's about understanding what the children need, creating a safe and supportive environment, and working with healthcare professionals to make sure the game is actually effective at reaching its goals. On top of that, working through the technical challenges of building an interactive application with animations, in a small team, strengthened my skills as a developer and designer.
